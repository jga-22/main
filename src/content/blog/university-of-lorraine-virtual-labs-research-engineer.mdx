In industry, digital twins are usually discussed in terms of performance, optimization, and operations. At the University of Lorraine, I saw another side of that story: the same ideas used to change how engineering is taught.

I worked as a **research engineer** on **Action 1.4 of the DUNE EOLE project**, an initiative focused on using **virtual labs in the factory of the future**. The question at the center of it: how can digital tools be used not only to simulate industrial systems, but also to transform the way engineering is taught?

## Changing the sequence

The project relied on two flexible manufacturing workshops and two collaborative robotics stations hosted by the University of Lorraine. Their **virtual twins** were designed to support immersive and more accessible learning experiences.

The pedagogical model behind it was a **flipped lab**. Instead of discovering everything for the first time on the physical equipment, students could first access digital learning spaces, explore the virtual twins of the systems, and understand the concepts through immersive tools before applying them on real installations.

Learning was no longer limited to physical access to the equipment. Students could prepare, explore, simulate, and then connect the concepts back to the real systems. The approach combined project-based learning, flipped-classroom dynamics, learning by doing, and immersive learning, and it made the student more active in the learning process.

I have always believed that deep learning happens when people can interact with a system, experiment with it, and build meaning through action, not only when they are told what it is.

## Five scenarios

The goal of Action 1.4 was to develop **five virtual training scenarios** from Bac+2 to Bac+5, centered on major themes of the factory of the future:

- industrial automation
- production line management and supervision
- industrial robotics
- collaborative robotics
- industrial maintenance

These themes reflect the real complexity of modern industry, and they required more than slides or standard practical sessions. The learning experience had to be more visual, more interactive, and more connected to real systems. That is where digital twins and 3D simulation became valuable.

## What I did

My role was not limited to one narrow technical task. I contributed to the operational development of the action, helping turn pedagogical ambition into usable deliverables: content, demonstrators, training materials, dashboards, and learning pathways across the different themes.

According to the activity report, the project resulted in around 50 deliverables, including 8 virtual labs, 5 virtual learning dashboards, 5 learning platforms, 21 identified practical sessions with pedagogical objectives, 5 learning pathways, and 4 pedagogical guides. Beyond development, the work also included teacher training, interventions in courses, and support for pedagogical projects.

That was a lot more than building a demo. It was about building an ecosystem.

The development was carried out with a broad network of pedagogical and technical contributors from different parts of the university, together with DITEX and with support from Dassault Systèmes. It reflected something I value in ambitious projects: meaningful outcomes come from combining **knowledge** and **know-how**. The academic side brings the learning objectives, the scientific perspective, and the pedagogical intent. The engineering side brings implementation, structure, tooling, and execution.

## Adoption comes after delivery

Creating innovative educational content is only one part of the challenge. The project was also concerned with **adoption**, **diffusion**, and **continuity**: once development was completed, the teaching community had to adopt the new materials, spread them across programs, and keep developing new virtual practical sessions over time.

Whether you build for education, industry, or digital transformation, success is rarely just about producing something technically impressive. It is about making sure it can live, spread, and be used by others. That requires training, documentation, communication, and a clear path for others to appropriate the work.

In that sense, the experience also taught me something about scalability. Not scalability in the cloud sense. Scalability in the human sense.

## A different use of the same tools

At the University of Lorraine, a virtual twin was no longer only an industrial asset. It also became a pedagogical asset. A simulation was no longer only for design validation: it could also be used to structure understanding. A dashboard was no longer only for monitoring: it could also become a learning space.

For me, this showed that technology becomes far more powerful when it is used not only to automate or optimize, but also to **enable learning**. The future of engineering is not only about more advanced systems. It is also about helping more people understand, use, and shape those systems.
