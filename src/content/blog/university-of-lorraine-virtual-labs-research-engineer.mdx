Most of my work with digital twins and virtual systems has been in industrial contexts — performance, optimization, production. At the University of Lorraine, I encountered the same tools being used for something fundamentally different: to change the structure of how students encounter complex industrial systems for the first time.

That difference mattered more than I expected.

## The DUNE EOLE project

I worked on **Action 1.4 of the DUNE EOLE project** as a **research engineer at the University of Lorraine**. The project's focus was **virtual labs in the factory of the future**, and it had access to real physical resources: two flexible manufacturing workshops and two collaborative robotics stations at the university, whose **virtual twins** would become the foundation for new pedagogical formats.

The ambition was not to replace physical labs. It was to change the sequence.

In traditional practical sessions, students arrive at the equipment and discover everything at once in front of the real machine. Time is limited, complexity is high, and the pace is often set by the equipment rather than by the student's understanding. The **flipped lab** approach inverts that. Students first explore the virtual twin, interact with the system digitally, and build conceptual grounding — before ever touching the hardware.

It sounds like a small change. In practice, it repositions the student from a passive receiver into someone who arrives already having made sense of the system. What happens on the physical equipment becomes confirmation and extension rather than first contact.

## Five scenarios for a complex field

The project developed five training scenarios spanning Bac+2 to Bac+5, organized around major Industry 4.0 themes:

- industrial automation
- production line management and supervision
- industrial robotics
- collaborative robotics
- industrial maintenance

Each scenario required more than converting an existing course. It required thinking about what kind of interaction made a concept learnable — what students needed to *do* to understand something, not just read or watch. That question drove a lot of the design choices across all five areas.

## On the scale of the work

When the project concluded, the activity report counted around 50 deliverables: 8 virtual labs, 5 learning dashboards, 5 learning platforms, 21 practical sessions with defined pedagogical objectives, 5 learning pathways, 4 pedagogical guides — along with teacher training, course interventions, and support for pedagogical projects.

That list can look like a production metric. But each of those deliverables was the result of a negotiation: between what was technically possible, what teachers wanted, what students actually needed, and what the university's infrastructure could support. Getting to 50 meant having a lot of those conversations, finding the right level of complexity each time, and building things that could outlast the project itself.

That is what turned this from a development effort into something closer to an ecosystem.

## The part that came after

One thing I did not fully anticipate was how much of the project's real challenge lay after the content was built.

Creating technically solid virtual labs is one problem. Getting teachers to adopt them, integrate them into their courses, and continue developing new scenarios once the project ends is a different one entirely. That requires training, documentation, and a clear path for others to appropriate the work and extend it.

It made me think differently about what scalability means.

In engineering, we usually think of it in terms of systems: infrastructure, compute, throughput. Here, scalability was entirely about people. How many teachers could use this? How many courses could it spread to? How long would it keep growing once we were gone? Those questions have no technical answers. They depend on whether the work is genuinely usable and genuinely wanted by the community it was built for.

That is a different kind of engineering problem — and in some ways a harder one.

## What the intersection opened up

What made this experience different from my other work with virtual systems was the purpose behind them.

In industry, a virtual twin is an operational asset — a tool for design, simulation, and monitoring performance. Here, the same kind of model became a **pedagogical** one. A 3D representation of a cobotics station in production becomes a space where a student can safely explore what happens when they change a parameter, break a sequence, or try something the physical machine would never allow.

That shifted something in how I thought about digital systems more broadly. The question stopped being only "how does this system perform?" and started including: "what does it make possible for the person using it?" Those are not the same question. And having worked in both contexts, I think the second one is often underestimated.
