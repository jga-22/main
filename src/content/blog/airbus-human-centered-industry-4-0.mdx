Eighty percent of the contributors to digital projects at Airbus Nantes believed they were already integrating end users during the design phase.

Only twenty percent of users felt their needs were well taken into account in the development of new tools.

That difference says a lot. It suggests the issue is not always bad intention or lack of effort. Sometimes, teams genuinely believe they are being user-centered, while users still experience the outcome very differently.

These figures come from the presentation summarizing my professional thesis at **Airbus Nantes**, on **the place of human-centered design in Industry 4.0**. They became the center of how I thought about the whole topic.

## The question behind the thesis

The thesis asked: **how can human-centered approaches contribute to the success of technological projects in the context of Industry 4.0?**

Industry 4.0 is often presented through technology: connected tools, data, AI, IoT, augmented reality, digital twins. What I progressively realized at Airbus is that technology is rarely the hardest part. The real challenge is often **adoption, understanding, alignment, and usability**.

A tool can be technically solid and still fail in practice if users do not see the value, do not feel involved, or do not find it usable in their daily work.

The context made this very concrete. Airbus Nantes brings together thousands of collaborators, multiple production lines, several product types, and an industrial ecosystem connected to larger Airbus programs. The digital ambition is real, and so is the pressure to make transformation useful on the ground. A digital solution there has to fit the rhythm of operations, the constraints of production, the diversity of users, and the reality of the shop floor.

## What the fieldwork showed

The thesis combined external research with local surveys and interviews carried out at Airbus Nantes.

Beyond the 20% / 80% gap, other findings pointed in the same direction: users wanted stronger support around training, clearer interfaces, and better integration into the design of new tools, even when operational constraints made that difficult.

For me, this was one of the most important lessons of the whole experience: **there is often a significant gap between designing for users and designing with users.**

## Three approaches, three parts of the gap

The thesis looked at three complementary approaches. In my reading, each one answers a different part of that gap.

**Design Thinking** helps teams better understand users, their contexts, their frustrations, and their expectations. It answers the first problem: if users feel unheard, the starting point is to look at their real situation instead of assuming it.

**Lean Startup** is about testing hypotheses early and learning before scaling. It answers the second problem: a solution can look right on paper and still not work for the people it was built for, so it is better to find out before deploying it widely.

**Agile/Scrum** means iterating and delivering progressively instead of waiting for a "perfect" solution. It answers the third problem: it keeps users in the loop throughout the project instead of only at the end.

Together, they offered a more operational path than simply saying "we should be more user-centric": understand people better, test earlier, learn faster, improve continuously, and involve users throughout the journey.

## Making it fit a large organization

One of the recommendations of the work was to integrate these human-centered principles into Airbus's project environment, by aligning them with the existing innovation and project logic rather than treating them as a parallel process.

That mattered, because in large industrial organizations good ideas do not survive on theory alone. They need to connect with governance, constraints, timing, investment logic, operational pressure, and the way projects are actually delivered.

So the challenge was never simply "apply design thinking." It was: **how do you adapt human-centered principles to an industrial system that is already structured, constrained, and moving at scale?**

## What I took from it

This experience reinforced my belief that the best transformations are not the ones that deploy the most technology, but the ones that create coherence between strategy and execution, tools and users, innovation and operations, digital ambition and human reality.

It also deepened my interest in digital transformation, industrial systems, enterprise architecture, and product thinking, and in the design of solutions that people can understand, trust, and use.

It was not only a thesis about Industry 4.0. It was an early lesson that technology creates the most value when it is designed around people, not only around possibilities.
