Eighty percent of the contributors to digital projects at Airbus Nantes believed they were already integrating end users during the design phase.

Twenty percent of the users felt their needs were actually taken into account.

That gap is not a measurement of bad faith. Most contributors genuinely believed they were doing the right things — running workshops, gathering requirements, involving stakeholders. The gap was not one of intention. It was one of method, scale, and the distance between what is done and what is felt.

That number became the center of everything I worked on during my time at **Airbus Nantes**, where I wrote my professional thesis on **the place of human-centered design in Industry 4.0**.

## The question behind the thesis

The thesis asked something that sounds straightforward: how can human-centered approaches contribute to the success of technological projects in Industry 4.0?

The easy answer is that you should involve users. Design with them, not just for them. Test earlier. Iterate faster.

What the Airbus context made concrete — in a way that theory does not fully capture — is how genuinely difficult that is inside a large industrial organization. Airbus Nantes is not a place where you introduce a methodology and watch it propagate. It is a site with thousands of collaborators, multiple production lines, complex programs, and a project culture built over decades to handle high-stakes delivery.

Digital transformation is happening there, seriously and with real ambition. But it happens inside a system that has its own logic, its own timelines, and its own operational pressure. That is the environment where human-centered ideas have to prove their worth. Not in a workshop. In a running industrial machine.

## What the fieldwork actually showed

The research combined literature with surveys and interviews conducted at the site.

Beyond the headline finding — 20% of users versus 80% of contributors — the pattern that emerged was more specific than "teams don't care about users." It was subtler.

Teams were integrating users the way they understood the concept, but that understanding was often too abstract to produce outcomes users recognized as real integration. A quarterly satisfaction survey is not the same as working alongside someone to understand where a tool fails them in practice. A requirements session is not the same as iterating with users through several versions until something genuinely clicks.

Users consistently asked for stronger support around training, clearer interfaces, and earlier involvement in design decisions. Not because the teams were ignoring them — but because the mechanisms for involvement were not creating actual influence over what got built.

The gap between those two levels of participation was where most of the problem lived.

## Three methodologies, three different parts of the problem

The fieldwork pointed to distinct failure modes — and the thesis proposed that different methodologies address each one specifically.

**Design Thinking** targets the earliest and most fundamental gap: understanding. The 20%/80% finding was not just a communication failure. It was a signal that teams were designing based on assumptions about users rather than observation of them. Design Thinking introduces structured practices for going further — contextual interviews, shadowing, co-design sessions — that make user reality visible before any solution is defined. In an industrial context like Airbus, where operational constraints are real and diverse across production lines, that kind of upstream understanding is not optional. It is what prevents building the wrong thing with the right intention.

**Lean Startup** targets the validation gap. Large organizations tend to build solutions at scale and deploy them broadly before knowing whether they work for actual users. The investment logic — big programs, long timelines, high-stakes delivery — makes this feel necessary. But it also means that failures are discovered late, when they are expensive to correct. Lean Startup introduces the discipline of testing hypotheses early, running small pilots before scaling, and treating deployment not as the end of a project but as the beginning of a learning loop. In a context where a failed digital tool can disrupt production or deepen resistance to future transformation, learning small is not a compromise. It is a risk reduction strategy.

**Agile/Scrum** targets the feedback gap inside development itself. Traditional project structures in large industry tend toward long delivery cycles — requirements gathered upfront, solution built in full, deployed at the end. Users see the tool for the first time when it is already finished. Agile breaks that cycle by delivering incrementally, keeping users in contact with the work throughout, and making course corrections possible before they become costly. In a digital transformation context, this means users can flag problems at sprint review rather than at go-live.

What mattered in the thesis was not proposing these three methods as a package deal, but showing how each one addresses a specific point in the chain where industrial digital projects tend to lose their users. Used together, they create continuity: understand before building, validate before scaling, iterate before finishing.

The harder challenge was never the frameworks themselves. It was the question of how to introduce them into a project environment that was already structured, constrained, and moving at scale. Good ideas do not survive large organizations on their own merits. They survive when someone figures out how to make them compatible with the system as it actually operates.

That is what the recommendation section of the thesis tried to address — not "here is why human-centered design is valuable," but "here is how it could connect with the way Airbus projects are actually run."

## Two things this experience changed

Looking back, this work shifted two things about how I think about transformation.

The first is a lasting skepticism toward solutions that seem obvious at the framework level but stay vague at the implementation level. Saying "we should be more user-centric" is easy. Explaining exactly what that changes in a sprint planning, a design review, or a deployment decision is harder — and that is where the actual work is.

The second is a respect for the people doing that work inside complex organizations. The contributors who showed up in my surveys with 80% confidence that they were integrating users were not wrong to believe that. They were working within the constraints of a large system and doing what those constraints allowed. Improving the system requires understanding its logic, not just criticizing its gaps.

That is what I still carry from Airbus: that meaningful change in industrial contexts happens at the intersection of ambition and operational reality, and bridging that intersection is almost always the hardest part of the job.
