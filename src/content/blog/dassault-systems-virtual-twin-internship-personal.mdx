**Poppy** is an open-source humanoid robot created at Inria. During my internship at **Dassault Systèmes**, I worked on a project around it, in collaboration with **ENSAM**.

The ambition was both technical and educational: to explore how the **3DEXPERIENCE platform** could help create a **virtual twin** of a robotic system, and how that twin could support design, control, simulation, and interaction.

Today, *digital twin* and *virtual twin* are everywhere. For me, back then, this was not a buzzword. It was the first time I could really see what it meant to build a bridge between the **physical world** and the **digital world**.

## A model that becomes operational

Before this experience, like many engineering students, I saw models mostly as tools for analysis, design, or validation.

This project pushed me to think differently. The virtual twin was not only there to represent the robot. It became part of the way we reasoned about movement, interaction, and system behavior. It connected what the robot **was** with what it **could do**.

A digital model is most powerful when it is treated not as static documentation but as something alive, something that can support decision-making, control, learning, and communication between systems and people.

## Two scenarios

The project was built around two scenarios, and it combined robot control, computer vision, human-machine interaction, and co-simulation between software environments.

**Poppy Parallel** focused on human-robot interaction, with different robot applications launched from a user interface.

## Control, vision, and interface

One part of the project focused on the **control system**. We worked on moving a point of the robot's hand to different positions, using the virtual twin to obtain inverse kinematics. A safety mechanism was also integrated to detect collisions and preserve the integrity of both the robot and the user.

Another part was **artificial vision**. We developed a color-based object recognition approach that enabled behaviors such as following an object with the head, and even using the detected object's movement to influence the position of the robot's arm.

This may sound simple compared with today's AI-heavy robotics landscape, but that is precisely why it was valuable. It taught me that elegant engineering is not always about using the most complex method. Sometimes it is about finding the right level of intelligence for the objective and integrating it well into the whole system.

We also built a **human-robot interface** that let users launch applications and monitor variables linked to actuator performance. That mattered more than it might seem: advanced technology becomes truly meaningful when it is accessible, observable, and usable by humans.

## Co-simulation

Finally, there was the **co-simulation** dimension. We used the **FMI (Functional Mock-up Interface)** concept to connect the 3DEXPERIENCE platform to the robot's actuators through Python tools such as **Pypot**.

This was one of the most formative aspects of the project for me, because it showed how much value can be created when different tools are made to cooperate instead of remaining isolated.

## Designing relationships

Looking back, the technical work was only part of the value. What I learned is that the future of engineering is not only about designing components. It is about designing **relationships**:

- between real systems and virtual systems
- between simulation and execution
- between algorithms and interfaces
- between technical rigor and human usability

A robot is a physical object. But once you connect it to models, interfaces, control logic, and simulation environments, it becomes something larger: a system of systems.

That internship helped shape my long-term interest in **digital continuity**, **industrial transformation**, **AI systems**, and **architecture**. Even though my work has evolved since then, I can still see the continuity, and the questions it left me with are still the ones I work on:

How do we connect tools instead of fragmenting them?
How do we make models useful beyond documentation?
How do we design technology that is technically strong, but also operationally meaningful?
