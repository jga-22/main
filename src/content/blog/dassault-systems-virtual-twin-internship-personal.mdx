There is a specific kind of engineering moment that is hard to describe to people who haven't experienced it: when two systems that have no business talking to each other suddenly do.

That moment came during my internship at **Dassault Systèmes**, working on a project around **Poppy** — an open-source humanoid robot created at Inria, in collaboration with **ENSAM** and Dassault Systèmes. The goal was to build a **virtual twin** of Poppy on the **3DEXPERIENCE platform** and use it to support control, simulation, and interaction. Not as a showcase. As something that actually worked.

Getting there was more complicated than it sounds.

## The virtual twin problem

Before this project, I understood digital twins conceptually. After it, I understood them operationally — which is a very different thing.

The challenge is not building a 3D model of a robot. The challenge is making that model behave in a way that is actually useful: receiving commands, returning state, responding to the physical system, and doing all of this across software environments that were not designed to cooperate.

The solution involved **FMI — Functional Mock-up Interface** — a standard for co-simulation that allowed us to connect the 3DEXPERIENCE platform with the robot's actuators through Python tools like **Pypot**. On paper, it is an elegant idea. In practice, it means spending a lot of time understanding what each system expects, why data formats don't quite match, and what happens when latency or timing assumptions collide.

That kind of work teaches you things no course does. You learn where the abstraction ends and where the real engineering starts.

## Two scenarios, two different problems

The project was structured around two scenarios.

**Poppy Parallel** focused on human-robot interaction. Different robot applications could be launched from a user interface, and the goal was to make the experience usable — not just functional, but observable and accessible to someone who is not writing the control code.

These two scenarios pulled in different directions. One asked: how do you design for a human user? The other asked: how do you make a model computationally useful? Working on both in parallel kept the project from becoming either purely a UI exercise or purely a control problem.

## The computer vision part

One of the more interesting pieces of work was the artificial vision module.

The approach was color-based: detect an object by its hue, track its position in the camera frame, and use that information to drive robot behavior — the head following the object, the arm responding to its movement.

By today's standards, this is not sophisticated computer vision. But I think that is precisely what made it valuable as a learning experience. It forced me to think carefully about the right level of complexity for the objective — not the most impressive technique, but the one that actually integrated well into the rest of the system. Elegant engineering is not always about using the most advanced method. Sometimes it is about choosing the right level of intelligence and making it work reliably within the whole.

## What the co-simulation really revealed

Of all the technical dimensions of this project, the FMI co-simulation work stayed with me the most.

Not because it was the hardest — though it was not easy — but because of what it showed about how engineering systems actually work when they have to cooperate.

Most tools are designed in isolation. They make assumptions about their environment, their data formats, their timing. When you try to connect them, those assumptions collide. Making two systems talk to each other is really a negotiation between two different sets of invisible constraints.

Getting the 3DEXPERIENCE platform and Pypot to exchange state through FMI meant understanding both systems well enough to broker that negotiation. It is slow, sometimes frustrating work. But it made me permanently interested in the question of **interoperability** — how tools can be made to cooperate without each one having to be redesigned from scratch.

That question has stayed relevant in every project I've worked on since.

## On models that do things

I came into this internship thinking of models mostly as representations: geometry, structure, behavior at rest. I left thinking of them as operational entities — things that participate in decisions.

A virtual twin is not a picture of a robot. At its best, it is a reasoning layer: a way of predicting behavior, computing trajectories, exposing state, and bridging physical and digital logic. It changes what you can do without touching the hardware. It changes how you test, how you iterate, and how you share understanding across a team.

That shift in how I thought about models is probably what I've carried furthest from this experience. Not a specific technique. A different way of asking what a model is *for* — and what it could become if you treated it as something active rather than descriptive.
